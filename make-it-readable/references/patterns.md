# The faults, and the move that repairs each one

Read this after reading the draft and before changing anything. Nine faults cover nearly every piece
that people abandon halfway. Each one here carries what it looks like, why it costs readers, and the
one structural move that fixes it, so every change made can be named in the change note.

Nothing in here licenses rewriting a sentence. Every repair is a move, a split, a grouping, a
heading, or a space.

## 1. The buried point

**Looks like** the piece opens on background, context, a definition or an apology, and the thing the
reader came for arrives in paragraph 4, 5 or 6. Very often that paragraph starts with "So really
what this means is" or "The short version is".

**Costs** the most readers of anything on this list. People decide within a few lines whether this is
for them, and a draft that spends those lines clearing its throat gets closed before the point.

**The move** is to lift that paragraph to the top, then read what is left of the old opening. Some of
it turns out to be support that belongs underneath, and some of it turns out to have been scaffolding
the writer needed and the reader never did. Leave the writer's sentence exactly as written, in its
new position.

## 2. The long run-up

**Looks like** a sentence or a paragraph whose only job is to announce the next one. "There are three
things to understand about pricing." "Let me explain why this matters."

**Costs** attention at exactly the moment it is most fragile, since the reader has been told they are
about to be told something, which is not the same as being told it.

**The move** is to delete the announcement and let the content that follows stand where it was. If the
run-up carried a number worth keeping, that number becomes the heading over the list.

## 3. The wall

**Looks like** a paragraph over about 6 lines on screen, usually covering more than one subject,
often with a "meanwhile" or an "although" at the hinge.

**Costs** readers through simple visual dread. A block with no way in reads as work before a word of
it has been read.

**The move** is to split at each change of subject. The hinge word is usually sitting right at the
break. Do not rewrite the halves to stand alone, just separate them.

## 4. Flat ground, no hierarchy

**Looks like** 2,000 words of equal-weight paragraphs, no headings, or headings so vague ("Some
thoughts", "Background") that they carry no information.

**Costs** everyone who wants to find their way back to one part, and everyone deciding from a skim
whether to invest. Without headings a reader cannot see the shape of the argument or judge the
distance to the end.

**The move** is to mark where the subject genuinely turns and put a heading there, built from words
already in the draft. The first line of each new section is nearly always sitting on the heading
already. Aim for headings that read as a summary of the piece when read on their own. Two or three
levels at most, because a fourth level is a filing system rather than a signpost.

## 5. Scattered relatives

**Looks like** the same subject coming up in paragraph 2, again in paragraph 7 and again near the end,
each time as if new.

**Costs** the reader the work of holding a half-finished thought open across the whole piece, which
is the tiring kind of reading nobody does voluntarily.

**The move** is to bring the pieces together under one heading, in the order the writer wrote them. If
one of the three genuinely belongs where it sits, because the argument needs it there, leave it and
group the other two.

## 6. The disguised list

**Looks like** a paragraph carrying three or more parallel items inside prose, usually joined by
"and also", "plus", "another thing", or a chain of semicolons.

**Costs** accuracy as much as attention, since a reader cannot count items in a paragraph and often
comes away with two of the four.

**The move** is to break the items out as a list, one per line, keeping each item in the writer's
words. Where the items have a natural order (steps in time, biggest to smallest), number them.
Where they do not, use bullets, because numbering unordered things suggests a ranking that is not
there.

## 7. The disguised comparison

**Looks like** several sentences setting two or three things against each other on the same few
points. "The first option is cheaper but slower, while the second costs more and arrives next day,
and the third..."

**Costs** the reader a mental spreadsheet they have to build and hold while continuing to read.

**The move** is a small table, one row per thing, one column per point of comparison. Only when there
are genuinely 2 or more things being compared on 2 or more shared points. A table with one real column
is a list wearing a costume.

## 8. Sprinkled white space

**Looks like** every line separated from every other by the same gap, or long stretches with no gap at
all. Both are the same fault, which is space carrying no meaning.

**Costs** the reader the free guidance that spacing gives when it is used deliberately, since things
placed close together are read as belonging together and things placed apart are read as separate.

**The move** is to make each gap mean something. Tight, no blank line between, for a question and its
answer, a statement and the detail that supports it, a cause and its effect. A full break for a new
thought, a change of subject, a shift in perspective. Then let one deliberate gap fall before the
line that matters most, because a line with room around it is the one the eye goes to first.

## 9. The same thing three times

**Looks like** a claim made in the opening, made again in the middle in slightly different words, and
made again at the end.

**Costs** trust rather than just time. Repetition reads as padding, and padding suggests the writer
did not have enough to say.

**The move** is to keep the strongest statement of it, in the writer's words, and cut the others.
Genuine repetition only. A point deliberately revisited after new evidence has arrived is doing real
work, and it stays.

## What to do when a fix wants new words

It will happen: two paragraphs join up and the seam wants a connecting phrase, or a lifted paragraph
opens on a "this" that no longer has anything to point at.

Reach for movement before prose. Usually a different cut point, or taking one more sentence along
with the paragraph, solves it with nothing added. Where a bridge is genuinely unavoidable, build it
from words already in the draft, keep it to the fewest possible, and list it in the change note as an
addition so the writer can approve it or write their own. A silent addition is the one thing that
makes a writer stop trusting the tool.
