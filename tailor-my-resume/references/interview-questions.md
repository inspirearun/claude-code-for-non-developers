# The interview: questions that work, and questions that do not

The aim is material a resume can use: a number, a date, a scale, a moment, a phrase in their own voice. Anything that comes back as a job description is a wasted question.

Ask one at a time. Follow what they get animated about rather than working down this list in order.

## Opening

Do not open with "tell me about your experience". You have their resume, and that is what it already says.

Open with something that puts them back in the room:

- "Take the job you had before this one. What did a bad Tuesday look like?"
- "Which part of that job would have fallen over if you had been away for a month?"
- "What did you fix that nobody asked you to fix?"

## Per bullet, the 5 that do the work

Take each existing bullet and ask, one at a time:

1. **Scale.** How many, how much, how often, for whom. "Managed the newsletter" and "managed a weekly newsletter to 40,000 subscribers" are different jobs.
2. **The before and after.** What was the state when they arrived, what was it when they left. This produces most of the real numbers, and it produces them honestly, because they are describing change rather than claiming credit.
3. **What broke.** Every long-held responsibility has a disaster in it. The disaster and what they did about it is usually the most hireable thing in the whole role and it is never on the resume.
4. **What they changed that outlasted them.** A process still running, a document still used, a hire still there. Durable work reads as senior.
5. **Who else was involved.** Alone, leading 4 people, or one of 12. Employers ask this in the room anyway, and a bullet that quietly implies a solo effort on team work is the easiest way to lose credibility fast.

## The press

Press once on anything soft, then take what you get.

| They say | Press with |
|---|---|
| "we grew it a lot" | "from what to what, over how long?" |
| "a big client" | "how big, and can we name them?" |
| "I streamlined the process" | "how long did it take before, and after?" |
| "a while back" | "which year?" |
| "I helped with" | "which part was yours?" |
| "we hit our targets" | "what was the target and what was the actual?" |

If the second answer is still soft, stop. Write the bullet without a number and move on. Pressing a third time produces an invented figure, and an invented figure is the one thing this whole skill exists to keep off the page.

## Questions that produce nothing

- "What are your strengths?" Produces adjectives. A resume cannot use an adjective.
- "What are you passionate about?" Produces the sentence they think you want.
- "Tell me about your leadership style." Produces a management book.
- Anything answerable with yes or no, unless it is a naming or permission question.
- Any question about a bullet you have already covered. Re-asking is the fastest way to lose someone's attention.

## Capturing it

Write down what they said, not a tidied version. "I was the only person who knew where anything was" survives into the resume and into the interview. "Served as the primary knowledge holder for the team" does not survive anywhere, because nobody says that out loud and it will not come back to them under pressure.

Keep a running file of everything that surfaced, including the material that will not fit on this resume. The next job may be the one where the discarded half is the relevant half.

## Before you finish

Two questions people rarely raise themselves, and both prevent damage:

- "Is there anything here you would rather not have written down?" A client under an agreement, a departure they would rather characterise themselves, a number the old employer treats as confidential.
- "Is there a gap in your dates?" Ask plainly and without any weight on it. A gap handled in one calm line is a non-event. A gap left for the reader to notice becomes the only thing they are thinking about.
