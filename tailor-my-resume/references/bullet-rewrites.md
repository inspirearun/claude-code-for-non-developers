# Rewriting bullets: before, after, and where the line is

Every bullet answers 3 questions in one sentence: what they did, at what scale, and what came of it. When the result genuinely does not exist, scale carries the bullet alone. When neither exists, the bullet is probably not worth its line.

## The shape

Start with the verb. Not "responsible for", which describes a job title rather than a person, and not "helped with", which invites the reader to guess how little.

Put the scale in early, because it is what stops a reader skimming. Put the result at the end, where it lands.

Keep it to one line where you can. A bullet running to 3 lines is a paragraph pretending, and paragraphs on a resume are read by nobody.

## Worked rewrites

**Before:** Managed social media accounts.

**Interview:** 4 accounts for a regional restaurant group, 11 locations. Instagram went from 3,000 to 26,000 over 2 years. She started posting the kitchen prep instead of the finished plates, which was her idea and nobody asked for it. Also built a posting calendar that the agency who came after her still uses.

**After:**
- Ran 4 social accounts for an 11-location restaurant group, growing Instagram from 3,000 to 26,000 followers in 2 years.
- Shifted the content from finished dishes to kitchen prep, a change I proposed, which tripled the average post engagement.
- Built the posting calendar the incoming agency still runs today.

Three bullets from one, and every part came from questions the resume never asked.

---

**Before:** Responsible for monthly reporting.

**Interview:** Pulled numbers from 3 systems into a spreadsheet, took 2 days every month. She automated most of it down to about 2 hours. The report went to the leadership team of 9. She cannot remember a specific decision that came out of it.

**After:**
- Owned the monthly performance report for a 9-person leadership team, pulling from 3 separate systems.
- Automated the collection, cutting it from 2 days a month to roughly 2 hours.

No outcome claimed for the report itself, because she could not name one. The automation number is the real result and it is strong enough on its own.

---

**Before:** Assisted with the CRM migration.

**Interview:** A team of 6 moved from a homegrown system to Salesforce. His part was the data: 40,000 customer records, deduplicating and mapping the fields. It went live 3 weeks late but with no data loss, which he says was not normal for that company.

**After:**
- Owned the data workstream on a 6-person CRM migration to Salesforce, mapping and deduplicating 40,000 customer records.
- Delivered the migration with no data loss.

"Assisted with" hid the fact that he owned a defined piece of it. The 3 weeks late is left out, which is a judgement call rather than a lie, because it was not his slippage and a resume is not a post mortem. If asked in the room, he says so.

## Where the honest version looks weaker and is still right

**The posting asks for:** experience managing a team.

**What she has:** she trained 2 new starters and reviewed their work for 6 months, with no reporting line.

**The inflated version:** Managed and developed a team of 2.

**The honest version:** Trained and reviewed the work of 2 new starters over their first 6 months.

The honest one looks smaller on the page. It also survives the question "and how did you handle their performance reviews?", which the inflated one does not. And a hiring manager reading the honest line sees someone who can describe their own scope accurately, which is itself a signal about how the rest of the resume should be read.

---

**The posting asks for:** SQL.

**What he has:** he has never written a query. He does use pivot tables on exports pulled by somebody else.

**The wrong answer:** putting SQL under Skills because the posting says so.

**The right answer:** leave SQL out entirely, let the data work he really did speak for itself, and put the gap in the notes file so he is ready for it. In a posting where SQL was under "nice to have", this costs him nothing at all.

## Keywords, and the actual boundary

The employer's vocabulary goes in wherever it describes work the person really did. If the posting says "stakeholder management" and she spent 3 years keeping 6 department heads aligned on one roadmap, write stakeholder management. She did the thing. Using her old company's word for it just makes the reader translate, and readers under time pressure do not translate.

Where the employer's word does not describe their work, leaving it out is the answer. There is no clever middle path. "Exposure to", "familiarity with" and "working knowledge of" are all read as "no", and they are read that way by the same person who will now wonder what else is being softened.

## Skills sections

Keep it to things that would survive a question. Tools they actually open, languages they actually speak, certifications they actually hold.

Cut the rating bars, the percentage scales and the star ratings. They are unverifiable by definition, they often live inside an image or a table where the text does not survive extraction, and nobody has ever been hired on a self-awarded 4 out of 5.
